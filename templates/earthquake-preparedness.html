Earthquake Preparedness Checklist: A Complete Guide for Families

📅 Published: May 2026 ⏱️ 15 min read 🖊️ RAA Safety Editorial

When an earthquake happens, you won't have time to think about what you should have done. You will only have time to react. Earthquake preparedness is the process of thinking ahead, identifying risks, and creating buffers that allow you to survive the shaking and manage the aftermath. This guide provides a detailed, granular checklist that every household in a seismic zone should implement.

1. The "72-Hour" Philosophy

Following a major earthquake, emergency services will be overwhelmed. Roads may be blocked, power lines may be down, and water systems may be compromised. Disaster relief agencies recommend that every household be prepared to survive independently for at least 72 hours. In reality, preparing for two weeks is ideal for large-scale urban disasters.

2. Strategic Home Securing

Most injuries during earthquakes are caused by falling objects, not collapsing buildings. "Earthquake-proofing" your home involves securing everything that can move or break.

  • The Kitchen: Install child-proof latches on all cabinets to prevent dishes and glassware from flying out. Ensure your refrigerator is anchored to the wall studs.
  • Living Areas: Secure tall furniture (bookshelves, entertainment centers) with L-brackets. Use museum wax or earthquake putty to secure small decorative items to shelves.
  • Sleeping Areas: Ensure nothing heavy (framed art, shelves) is mounted above where you sleep. Keep a pair of sturdy shoes and a flashlight in a bag tied to the leg of your bed.
  • The Water Heater: A major source of fire and flood. Ensure it is strapped with heavy-duty metal bands to the wall studs.

3. The Essential Emergency Kit

Divide your supplies into two categories: a "Stay-at-Home" kit and a "Go-Bag" for evacuation.

The Survival Essentials

  • Water: 1 gallon per person per day (for drinking and sanitation).
  • Food: Canned goods, protein bars, and comfort foods. Don't forget a manual can opener.
  • Lighting: Flashlights (LED preferred) and headlamps for every family member.
  • Radio: A hand-crank or battery-powered NOAA weather radio.

Health & Communication

  • Medications: A 7-day supply of personal prescriptions.
  • First Aid: Bandages, antiseptics, gloves, and a first-aid manual.
  • Power: Fully charged portable power banks for mobile phones.
  • Documents: Copies of IDs, insurance, and medical records in a waterproof bag.

4. The Communication & Action Plan

Panic is the enemy of safety. A clear, practiced plan is the antidote.

Designate Internal Safe Spots

Identify a "safe spot" in every room — usually under a sturdy table or away from windows and exterior walls. Practice moving to these spots so it becomes an automatic reflex.

The Out-of-Area Contact

Following a major event, local phone lines are often congested. However, long-distance calls may still go through. Designate a relative or friend living in another state or country as your family's check-in point. Every family member should memorize this person's phone number.

Meeting Points

Designate two meeting points: one right outside your home (in case of fire) and one outside your neighborhood (in case of mandatory evacuation).

5. Financial & Digital Preparedness

A often overlooked part of preparedness is the ability to recover financially. Ensure you have some cash in small denominations stored in your emergency kit, as ATMs and credit card machines will not work without power. Take photos of every room in your house and save them to a cloud-based folder for insurance purposes.

Conclusion: A Prepared Community is a Safe Community

Earthquake preparedness is not a one-time task; it is a lifestyle. Review your supplies every six months (when you change your clocks), check expiration dates on food and batteries, and run a family drill. By taking these steps today, you are giving yourself and your loved ones the best possible chance of a safe outcome.

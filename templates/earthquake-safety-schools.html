Earthquake Safety for Schools: Protecting the Future

📅 Published: May 2026 ⏱️ 11 min read 🖊️ RAA Education Outreach

Schools are the heart of any community. Because they are densely populated with students and educators, they require specialized earthquake safety protocols. Ensuring that a school is "earthquake-ready" involves a combination of structural assessment, rigorous drill practice, and psychological preparation for the children. This guide provides a framework for students, teachers, and parents to build a safer school environment.

1. Preparing the Classroom Environment

Safety begins in the classroom. Teachers should conduct a monthly "hazard hunt" to identify and eliminate risks before an earthquake occurs.

  • Anchor Everything: Bookshelves, filing cabinets, and heavy equipment (like computers or science tools) must be bolted to wall studs.
  • Safe Seating: Desks should be positioned away from large windows, mirrors, or glass-fronted cabinets that could shatter during shaking.
  • Exit Paths: Ensure that all doorways and aisles are kept clear of backpacks, strollers, or furniture that could block a rapid evacuation.

2. Teaching "Drop, Cover, and Hold On"

The "Drop, Cover, and Hold On" technique is the most important skill a student can learn. In a school setting, the protocol is specialized:

  • Under the Desk: Students should drop to their knees under their desks. One hand should be used to cover the back of the neck, and the other should hold on firmly to a desk leg.
  • The Wheelbase: For students in wheelchairs, the protocol is "Lock, Cover, and Hold." Lock the wheels, cover the head and neck with a book or bag, and hold on to the chair frame.
  • The Silent Classroom: During a drill, students should be trained to remain silent. This allows them to hear instructions from the teacher or emergency broadcasts.
Training Tip: Use "The Great ShakeOut" resources to make drills engaging and informative for younger children without causing unnecessary fear.

3. The Three Phases of a School Drill

A comprehensive school earthquake drill should be practiced at least once every semester and include three phases:

  1. The Shaking Phase: Everyone drops, covers, and holds on. Teachers should time this for at least 60 seconds (simulating a major event).
  2. The Evacuation Phase: Once the shaking stops, the evacuation begins. Students should move in an orderly line, using the "buddy system," toward a designated assembly area (usually the middle of a sports field or open parking lot).
  3. The Roll Call Phase: At the assembly area, teachers must take attendance immediately to ensure all students are accounted for. Designated "Search and Rescue" teams of trained staff check the building.

4. The Role of Parents: The Reunification Plan

One of the biggest challenges after a school-hour earthquake is the safe reunification of students and parents. Schools must have a strict "Check-Out" policy. Parents should be informed that children will only be released to people listed on their emergency contact cards. This prevents confusion and potential safety risks during the chaos of a disaster.

5. Post-Earthquake Support

Earthquakes can be traumatic, especially for younger students. After the physical danger has passed, schools should be prepared to provide psychological support. Clear communication from teachers and a return to regular routines as soon as safely possible can help children feel secure again.

Conclusion

School safety is a shared responsibility. When students are empowered with knowledge and teachers are equipped with clear protocols, the entire community becomes more resilient. By turning drills into a natural part of the school year, we ensure that our children are ready to respond correctly, calmly, and safely if the ground ever begins to move.

🔬 Earthquake Science & Seismology

Earthquakes occur due to the dynamic motion of Earth's tectonic plates. The Earth's outer crust is fractured into large puzzle-like slabs called lithospheric plates that drift slowly over the semi-fluid asthenosphere beneath. Along plate boundaries (faults), the edges of plates get stuck due to friction while the rest of the plates continue moving.

Over decades or centuries, immense elastic strain builds up in the locked rocks. When this stress finally exceeds the frictional strength of the fault, the rocks rupture violently, slipping rapidly and releasing the stored energy as seismic waves that travel through the planet and shake the surface.

No. No scientific organization in the world (including USGS, IMD, or EMSC) can predict the exact date, time, and location of an earthquake. Any claims of exact predictions are scientifically unsubstantiated.

What seismologists can do is calculate probabilistic seismic hazard assessments. This means scientists determine the probability of a certain magnitude earthquake occurring in a specific region over a multi-decade span (e.g., a 60% probability of a M6.7+ event in Northern California over 30 years). Early warning systems can also detect the initial P-waves seconds before stronger S-waves arrive, giving citizens and automated systems seconds of crucial warning.

The two terms describe distinct spatial points of a seismic event:

  • Hypocenter (Focus): The true 3D point inside the Earth's crust where the rupture starts and initial energy is released. Depth is measured from the surface down to this point.
  • Epicenter: The geographical point on the Earth's surface directly vertically above the hypocenter. This is the coordinate (latitude and longitude) mapped on geographical maps.

Charles Richter developed the Richter Scale in 1935 specifically for measuring moderate earthquakes in Southern California using Wood-Anderson seismographs. It "saturates" (fails to accurately distinguish size) for earthquakes larger than magnitude 7.0 and cannot properly measure distant or deep events.

Today, modern seismological agencies rely on the Moment Magnitude Scale (Mw). Moment magnitude is based on the physical parameters of the fault rupture: the shear modulus of the rock, the surface area of the fault that slipped, and the average distance of the slip. Because it directly quantifies energy release without saturation, it accurately measures events of all sizes worldwide.

While often confused in news reports, magnitude and intensity measure two completely different characteristics:

  • Magnitude (Mw): Represents the singular, fixed quantity of energy released at the earthquake source. It does not change with distance.
  • Intensity (e.g., Modified Mercalli Intensity - MMI): Measures the localized strength of ground shaking and observed physical damage at a given location. Intensity varies greatly depending on distance from the epicenter, focal depth, local soil composition (e.g., bedrock vs. soft sedimentary basin), and structural design.

These terms describe relative sequences of earthquakes in a specific cluster:

  • Mainshock: The largest earthquake in a sequence.
  • Foreshock: An earthquake that occurs before a larger mainshock in the same geographic area. An event can only be identified as a foreshock retroactively once a larger mainshock occurs.
  • Aftershocks: Smaller earthquakes that occur in the same area during the days, weeks, months, or years following a mainshock as the crust adjusts to the altered stress state.

🛡️ Safety & Preparedness

The globally accepted, research-backed response recommended by disaster agencies (NDMA, USGS, FEMA, Red Cross) is:

  1. DROP: Drop down onto your hands and knees. This position protects you from being knocked over and allows you to stay low while crawling if necessary.
  2. COVER: Cover your head and neck (and your entire body if possible) underneath a sturdy table or desk. If no shelter is nearby, drop next to an interior wall and cover your head with your arms.
  3. HOLD ON: Hold on to your shelter with one hand and move with it if it shifts, keeping your other arm shielding your neck and head until shaking completely ceases.

No. These are outdated myths:

  • Doorways: In modern construction, doorways are no stronger than other parts of a house and do not protect against flying or falling debris. Furthermore, a swinging door can cause severe injury.
  • Running outside: Research shows that attempting to run while ground is shaking is one of the leading causes of injury due to falling, collapsing exterior facades, shattering glass windows, and falling power lines. Stay inside until shaking stops, then evacuate calmly.

A basic 72-hour disaster supply kit should contain:

  • Water: One gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day for drinking and sanitation
  • Food: A 3-day supply of non-perishable, easy-to-prepare food with a manual can opener
  • Battery-powered or hand-crank emergency radio (NOAA/national weather broadcasts)
  • LED flashlight and extra batteries
  • First aid kit including essential prescription medications
  • Whistle to signal for search and rescue
  • Dust mask, plastic sheeting, and duct tape to help filter contaminated air
  • Sturdy shoes, work gloves, and copies of important identification documents in a waterproof container

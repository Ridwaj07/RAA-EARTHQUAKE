How Earthquakes Are Measured: A Guide to Magnitude and Intensity

📅 Updated: May 2026 ⏱️ 10 min read 🖊️ By RAA Science Team

When a major earthquake strikes, the first question people often ask is, "What was the magnitude?" However, magnitude is only one part of the story. In seismology, we use a variety of scales and instruments to quantify seismic events, ranging from the energy released at the source to the actual damage experienced on the surface. Understanding these measurements is essential for interpreting earthquake news and assessing personal risk.

1. The Instrument: How Seismographs Work

At the center of earthquake measurement is the Seismograph (or seismometer). This instrument records the ground's motion caused by seismic waves. A traditional seismograph consists of a weight suspended by a spring or wire. When the ground shakes, the frame of the instrument moves with it, while the heavy weight remains relatively stationary due to inertia. A pen attached to the weight records the movement on a rotating drum of paper, creating a Seismogram.

Modern seismometers are digital and infinitely more sensitive. They can detect the slight vibrations of an earthquake occurring on the opposite side of the planet. These instruments use electronic sensors to record velocity or acceleration, which is then processed by computers to determine an earthquake's characteristics.

2. Quantifying Energy: Magnitude Scales

Magnitude represents the size of the earthquake at its source. It is an "objective" measurement of the energy released. There are several magnitude scales, each with its own specific use cases.

The Richter Scale (Local Magnitude, ML)

Developed in 1935 by Charles Richter and Beno Gutenberg, the Richter Scale was the first widely used method to quantify earthquakes. It measures the amplitude of the largest seismic wave recorded on a specific type of seismograph. However, the Richter Scale has limitations: it is only accurate for smaller, local earthquakes and "saturates" for massive events (above M7.0), meaning it cannot distinguish between very large magnitudes accurately.

The Moment Magnitude Scale (MW)

Most scientists today use the Moment Magnitude Scale (MW) for media reports and scientific analysis of large earthquakes. Unlike the Richter Scale, which measures wave amplitude, MW measures the "Seismic Moment" — a combination of the area of the fault that ruptured, the average amount of slip (movement) on the fault, and the rigidity of the rocks.

The Moment Magnitude scale is logarithmic. This means that each whole number increase on the scale represents a 10-fold increase in the amplitude of the waves and approximately a 32-fold increase in the energy released.

Energy Comparison: A magnitude 7.0 earthquake releases about 31.6 times more energy than a 6.0, and about 1,000 times more energy than a 5.0 (31.6 x 31.6).

3. Quantifying Impact: The Intensity Scale

While magnitude is a fixed number for an earthquake, Intensity is a subjective measure that varies based on your location. It describes the physical effects of the shaking on people, buildings, and the geography.

The Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale (MMI)

The MMI scale uses Roman numerals from I to XII to categorize the impact of an earthquake:

  • MMI I: Not felt except by a very few under especially favorable conditions.
  • MMI IV: Felt indoors by many, outdoors by few. Dishes, windows, doors disturbed; walls make cracking sound.
  • MMI VI: Felt by all, many frightened. Some heavy furniture moved; a few instances of fallen plaster. Damage slight.
  • MMI VIII: Damage slight in specially designed structures; considerable damage in ordinary substantial buildings with partial collapse.
  • MMI XII: Damage total. Waves seen on ground surfaces. Objects thrown upward into the air.

Comparison: Magnitude vs. Intensity

Feature Magnitude (MW) Intensity (MMI)
Definition Energy released at source Observed effects on surface
Number per EQ One fixed number Varies by location
Factors Fault area, slip, rock rigidity Distance, soil type, building quality

Why Measurement Matters for Safety

Measurement is not just for the textbooks. It informs building codes, land-use planning, and emergency response. Engineering "earthquake-resistant" buildings requires a precise understanding of the ground acceleration (measured in 'g') that an earthquake of a certain magnitude and intensity will produce in a specific area. By measuring and recording every tremor, we create a data-driven map of our world's seismic risks.

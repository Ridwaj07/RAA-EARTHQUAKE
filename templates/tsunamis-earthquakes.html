Giant Waves: The Destructive Connection Between Earthquakes and Tsunamis

📅 Published: May 2026 ⏱️ 13 min read 🖊️ RAA Oceanography Desk

A tsunami is one of nature's most terrifying and destructive forces. Often incorrectly called "tidal waves," tsunamis have nothing to do with the tides. Instead, the majority of the world's most catastrophic tsunamis are triggered by major undersea earthquakes. Understanding this relationship is critical for anyone living in or visiting coastal regions.

1. The Mechanism: How an Earthquake Starts a Tsunami

Not every earthquake creates a tsunami. For an earthquake to trigger a massive ocean wave, it must meet three specific criteria:

  • Location: The epicenter must be beneath the ocean floor or very close to the coast.
  • Magnitude: The earthquake must be powerful, typically at least a Magnitude 7.0 or greater.
  • Motion: The earthquake must involve vertical displacement of the seafloor.

When a subducting tectonic plate snaps upward during a "megathrust" earthquake, it pushes the entire column of water above it. This sudden displacement creates a series of waves that travel outward in all directions across the ocean.

An Analogy: Think of a tsunami like dropping a large stone in a pond. The "stone" is the rising seabed, and the ripples are the tsunami waves.

2. The Travel: Speed and Behavior in the Open Ocean

In the deep ocean, tsunamis are almost invisible. They may be only a few inches high and have wavelengths of hundreds of miles. Ships at sea often do not even notice a tsunami passing beneath them. However, these waves travel at incredible speeds — as fast as a jet plane (over 800 km/h).

As the tsunami approaches the shallow water near the coast, it slows down significantly. However, because the energy of the wave has nowhere else to go, the wave height increases dramatically in a process called "shoaling." A 1-foot wave in the deep ocean can become a 30-foot (or higher) wall of water at the coast.

3. Tsunami Warnings: Natural and Technical

If you are on the coast, you may have two types of warnings:

Technical Warnings

The Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC) and other agencies use undersea pressure sensors and coastal tide gauges to detect tsunamis. If a wave is detected, they broadcast alerts to local governments to begin evacuations. However, if the earthquake is very close to the coast, the tsunami may arrive before the technical warning can be issued.

Natural Warnings (The most important)

If you feel any of these, do not wait for an official siren. Move to high ground immediately:

  • Strong Shaking: If the ground shakes so hard that it's difficult to stand, a tsunami may be coming.
  • Ocean Withdrawal: Sometimes, the ocean will pull back dramatically before a tsunami arrives, exposing the seafloor and fish. Never go onto the beach to investigate this.
  • The Roar: People who have survived tsunamis often describe a loud, thunderous roar similar to a jet engine or a freight train coming from the ocean.

4. Survival Strategy: What to Do

If a tsunami is suspected:

  1. Move Inland and Upward: Head to ground that is at least 100 feet (30m) above sea level or 2 miles (3km) inland.
  2. Use Your Legs: Traffic jams are common during evacuations. If possible, evacuate on foot.
  3. Stay There: A tsunami is not just one wave; it is a "train" of waves that can arrive hours apart. Sometimes the second or third wave is larger than the first. Do not return to the coast until authorities give the official "all clear."

Conclusion

The connection between earthquakes and tsunamis is one of the most powerful examples of how our planet's internal movements change its surface. While tsunamis are devastating, they are also survivable if you understand the warning signs and react immediately. Remember: if you feel the Earth shake at the coast, high ground is your only priority.
